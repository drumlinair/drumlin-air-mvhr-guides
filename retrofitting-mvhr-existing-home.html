Retrofitting MVHR into an Existing Home

Retrofitting Mechanical Ventilation with Heat Recovery into an existing home is different from planning it for a new build. In a new home, ductwork can be designed into the structure from the start. In an existing home, the installer must work with the rooms, ceilings, roof spaces and services that are already there, while protecting finishes and finding practical routes for the unit and ductwork. That does not mean retrofit MVHR is unsuitable, but it does mean the survey, design and installation stages need extra care. This guide explains what to consider, what questions to ask, and how a retrofit project is usually approached, so that homeowners can judge whether it fits their home and their renovation plans.

Why existing homes need a careful survey

Every existing home is different. Its age, construction, insulation, airtightness, heating system, window type and previous renovations all affect how MVHR will work. A home that has been well insulated and made more airtight may benefit more from planned ventilation, because natural draughts no longer provide the same air change. A home that is still draughty may need airtightness work, insulation work or other preparatory steps before MVHR can work as intended, because the system relies on controlled supply and extract rather than uncontrolled leakage. A survey should look at ceiling voids, roof spaces, joist directions, stairwells, cupboards, utility areas and possible plant locations, as well as the condition of existing finishes. It should also consider how air will move between rooms, whether internal doors allow transfer, and where external intake and exhaust terminals can be placed.

Finding routes for ductwork

Ductwork is often the biggest practical challenge in a retrofit. In some homes, there are generous ceiling voids, a usable attic or a service area that can carry ducts without major disruption. In others, routes must be created through bulkheads, wardrobes, cupboards or a new ceiling detail, and the design must balance airflow, noise and appearance. Short, direct routes are preferable, but retrofit routes may need to follow the available space rather than the ideal line. Duct sizes, bends, supports and insulation should still be planned, because a poorly routed system can be noisy, difficult to commission or hard to service. The design should also leave access for cleaning, inspection and filter changes, and it should show where valves will be located in each room. Homeowners should ask how much making good is included, what finishes will be disturbed, and how the finished ductwork and valves will look in living spaces.

Airtightness, moisture and indoor air quality

MVHR supplies fresh, filtered air to living rooms and bedrooms and extracts stale, humid air from kitchens, bathrooms and utility rooms. Heat from the outgoing air is transferred to the incoming air through a heat exchanger, with heat recovery commonly described in the range of 70 to 90 percent depending on the unit, the installation and the operating conditions. In an existing home, that process can help with moisture control, cooking odours and stuffiness, provided the system is balanced and maintained. However, MVHR is not a substitute for addressing damp, leaks or other building defects, and it should not be used to mask a moisture problem that needs building work. A retrofit assessment should therefore consider condensation, mould, existing extractor fans, cooker hoods, fireplaces, stoves and other ventilation openings, so that the new system works with the home rather than against it. A summer bypass, where fitted, can help in warmer conditions by bypassing the heat exchanger when extra heat is not wanted.

Phasing, commissioning and living with the system

Some retrofit projects are carried out as part of a wider renovation, when ceilings are open and ductwork can be installed with less disruption. Others are carried out as a standalone project, where the installer works room by room and protects existing finishes as far as possible. Either way, room-by-room commissioning is important after installation, because it checks the airflow at each supply and extract point against the design and allows adjustments to be made. Homeowners should receive a clear explanation of the controls, including normal and boost settings, filter care and servicing, so that the system is used correctly. Filters need attention in line with the manufacturer guidance, and the unit should remain accessible for servicing.
