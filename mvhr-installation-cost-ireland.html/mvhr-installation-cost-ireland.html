MVHR Installation Cost Guide for Ireland

Mechanical Ventilation with Heat Recovery, known as MVHR, is a whole-home ventilation system that supplies fresh, filtered air to living rooms and bedrooms while extracting stale, humid air from kitchens, bathrooms and utility rooms. As the two air streams pass through a heat exchanger, warmth from the outgoing air can be transferred to the incoming air, with heat recovery commonly described in the range of 70 to 90 percent depending on the unit, the installation and the operating conditions. That principle helps explain why MVHR is often discussed for well-insulated and airtight homes, where uncontrolled draughts are limited and a planned ventilation route matters more than it does in a leaky older building. This guide explains what shapes the cost of an MVHR installation in Ireland, without giving a single price that could mislead a reader whose home, layout and requirements are different.

Why there is no single MVHR price

Two homes can look similar from the outside and still need very different MVHR work. The number of rooms, the floor area, the number of storeys, the ceiling heights, the position of the unit, the length and complexity of the duct runs, and the ease of access to roof spaces, service voids and plant areas all affect the scope of work. A compact single-storey home with a straightforward duct layout is a different project from a larger two-storey home, a home with rooms spread across several levels, or a home where ductwork must be threaded carefully around existing structure, services and finishes. The type of MVHR unit, its airflow capacity, its controls, its filter arrangement and features such as a summer bypass also form part of the specification. For that reason, a responsible cost discussion starts with the home itself, not with a headline figure, and it should separate the equipment, the ductwork, the controls, the commissioning and any related building work so the reader can see what is included.

What a typical scope includes

A complete MVHR installation normally includes design work, the MVHR unit, supply and extract ductwork, valves and grilles, controls, and commissioning. Design-stage ductwork planning is important because duct routes, duct sizes and valve positions affect airflow, noise, pressure and ease of maintenance. Short, direct and well-supported duct runs are generally preferable to long, convoluted routes, and careful planning at design stage can reduce the need for changes once work is under way. After installation, room-by-room commissioning checks the airflow at each supply and extract point against the design intent, so that living spaces receive fresh air and wet rooms are properly extracted. Servicing is also part of the longer-term picture, because filters need attention and the unit and ductwork should remain accessible for inspection and maintenance. Readers comparing quotations should check whether each of these elements is included, and whether controls, filters and commissioning are described clearly, rather than comparing headline totals alone.

New build, renovation and retrofit differences

MVHR is often simplest to plan in a new build or a major renovation, where ductwork can be integrated into the structure, ceiling voids and service routes before finishes are applied. In an existing home, retrofitting MVHR can still be considered, but the available routes for ductwork, the space for the unit, and the condition of the existing building may add complexity. Access constraints, the need to protect existing finishes, and the work needed to make the home sufficiently airtight for MVHR to work as intended can all influence scope. A retrofit assessment should therefore look at the whole home, including how air moves between rooms, where internal doors and transfer routes sit, and where external terminals can be placed without creating short-circuiting between intake and exhaust. Those practical questions help determine whether a full whole-home system, a phased approach or further preparatory work is the sensible starting point.

Running costs and longer-term considerations

Installation cost is only one part of the picture. An MVHR unit uses electricity to run its fans, and its filters need periodic replacement or cleaning in line with the manufacturer guidance for the unit. Heat recovery can reduce the amount of heat lost through ventilation compared with opening windows or relying on uncontrolled infiltration, but the actual effect depends on the home, its heating system, its airtightness, how the system is used and how well it is maintained. A summer bypass, where fitted, allows the system to bypass the heat exchanger in warmer conditions, so that incoming air is not unnecessarily warmed when the home does not need that heat. Readers should also consider noise, control settings, boost functions for kitchens and bathrooms, and frost protection in cold weather, because these practical details affect day-to-day comfort as much as the initial specification. A clear explanation of how to use the controls, when to change filters and who to contact for servicing will help the system continue to perform as designed.

Questions to ask before deciding

Before choosing an installer, it is helpful to ask how the design was calculated, what airflow rates are intended for each room, where the unit and ductwork will be located, how ductwork will be accessed for cleaning and servicing, what commissioning will be carried out room by room, and what ongoing servicing is recommended. It is also helpful to ask what building work, electrical work or making good is included, and what is excluded, so that there are no surprises later. Floor plans, information about the construction and insulation, and details of any planned extensions or renovations can help an installer give a more accurate project-specific assessment.
