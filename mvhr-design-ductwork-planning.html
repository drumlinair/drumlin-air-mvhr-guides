MVHR Design and Ductwork Planning

Good MVHR performance starts long before a unit is fitted. Mechanical Ventilation with Heat Recovery works as a planned system, not as a single box in a utility room, so the design must connect the unit, the ductwork, the rooms and the controls into one coherent layout. Fresh air is supplied to living rooms and bedrooms, stale air is extracted from kitchens, bathrooms and utility rooms, and heat from the outgoing air is transferred to the incoming air through a heat exchanger, with heat recovery commonly described in the range of 70 to 90 percent depending on the unit, the installation and the operating conditions. When that layout is planned at design stage, airflow, noise, pressure, maintenance access and comfort can all be considered together, instead of being left to be resolved on site when walls and ceilings are already closed.

Starting with the rooms, not the unit

Design usually begins with the home itself: its floor plans, room sizes, room uses, ceiling heights, number of storeys and the way people move between rooms. Living rooms and bedrooms need a steady supply of fresh air, while wet rooms need effective extraction to remove moisture and odours. The air that is supplied to living spaces should be able to move through the home toward the extract points, often through door undercuts or transfer routes, without being blocked by tightly sealed internal doors. A room-by-room approach therefore matters, because a whole-home total airflow figure on its own does not show whether a particular bedroom is under-supplied or a particular bathroom is under-extracted. Design-stage planning should record the intended airflow for each room, so that the later commissioning stage can check each supply and extract valve against that intention.

Planning duct routes

Ductwork planning is where many MVHR designs succeed or struggle. Short, direct routes with gentle bends generally help keep pressure losses and noise lower than long, winding routes with many sharp turns. Ducts need a clear path through ceiling voids, service risers, roof spaces or purpose-built bulkheads, and they must be supported properly so they do not sag, kink or vibrate. Duct sizes matter too, because a duct that is too small for its airflow can create noise and resistance, while an oversized duct may be difficult to route. Planners also need to think about insulation for ducts in cold or unheated spaces, about condensation control, and about keeping supply and extract routes separate and clearly identified. Where ductwork passes through fire or acoustic boundaries, the design should consider the relevant construction details, rather than leaving those decisions to the installation stage.

Choosing the unit location and external terminals

The MVHR unit needs a location that is accessible for filter changes, servicing and inspection, that has suitable power and drainage where required, and that allows ductwork to reach all parts of the home without excessive lengths. Common locations include a utility room, a plant area or a roof space, but each option has trade-offs in terms of noise, access, temperature and duct lengths. External terminals also need careful placement. The fresh-air intake and the stale-air exhaust should be positioned so that exhaust air is not drawn straight back into the intake, and so that terminals are clear of obstructions, prevailing wind effects and sources of pollution where possible. The design should show both terminals, the routes to them and any access needed for cleaning or inspection.

Controls, summer bypass and commissioning

Controls allow occupants to adjust ventilation for normal use and for boost periods, for example when cooking or showering. A summer bypass, where fitted, allows incoming air to bypass the heat exchanger in warmer conditions, so that heat is not added to the incoming air when it is not wanted. Design should explain how controls will be used, where controllers will be located, and how boost functions will operate, because a system that occupants do not understand is less likely to be used well. After installation, room-by-room commissioning measures the airflow at each valve, adjusts dampers or valves where needed, and records the results. That step turns a design intention into a verified installation, and it gives the homeowner a clear record of what was set and why. Servicing should also be planned at design stage, with access left for the unit, filters and ductwork, rather than being treated as an afterthought.

Working with the building team

MVHR design works best when it is coordinated with the architect, builder, heating installer and electrician. Changes to room layouts, ceiling heights, joist directions or service routes can all affect ductwork, so early coordination reduces the risk of clashes and late changes. For a new build or a major renovation, design-stage ductwork planning can be included before first-fix work, which helps keep routes tidy and accessible. For a retrofit, the design must work around existing structure, and a careful survey of available voids and routes is essential.
