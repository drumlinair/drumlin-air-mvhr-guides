Ventilation, Part F and NZEB in Airtight Homes

Modern homes in Ireland are built to be more energy efficient than homes of previous generations. Better insulation, better windows and more careful construction reduce heat loss, and the move toward Nearly Zero Energy Buildings, known as NZEB, is part of that wider change. At the same time, a home still needs a reliable supply of fresh air and a reliable way to remove stale, humid air. That is where ventilation design becomes central, and where Part F and Part L of the Building Regulations are often discussed together. Part F deals with ventilation, while Part L deals with the conservation of fuel and energy. Together, they frame a simple idea: a home should be energy efficient, but it should not achieve that efficiency by trapping stale air, moisture and pollutants inside.

Why airtight homes need planned ventilation

In an older, leaky home, air often entered and left through gaps around windows, doors, floors and roofs. That uncontrolled air change provided some ventilation, but it also wasted heat and could create draughts and cold spots. In an airtight home, those gaps are deliberately reduced, so ventilation can no longer be left to chance. Without a planned system, an airtight home can become stuffy, humid air from cooking, showering and drying clothes can build up, and condensation can appear on cold surfaces. Planned ventilation replaces uncontrolled leakage with a controlled route: fresh, filtered air is supplied to living rooms and bedrooms, and stale air is extracted from kitchens, bathrooms and utility rooms. Air can then move through the home in a predictable way, from cleaner rooms toward wetter rooms, rather than through random gaps.

How MVHR fits into this picture

Mechanical Ventilation with Heat Recovery is one form of planned, continuous ventilation. It supplies and extracts air mechanically, and it transfers heat from the outgoing air to the incoming air through a heat exchanger, with heat recovery commonly described in the range of 70 to 90 percent depending on the unit, the installation and the operating conditions. That heat recovery is important in an airtight, well-insulated home, because it allows fresh air to be provided without losing as much of the heat that the home has already paid to produce, compared with simply opening windows in cold weather. A summer bypass, where fitted, allows the system to bypass the heat exchanger in warmer conditions, so that incoming air is not unnecessarily warmed. MVHR does not replace good insulation, good airtightness detailing or a suitable heating system, but it complements them by making ventilation a designed part of the home, rather than an accident of its construction.

Design, commissioning and the building process

For a new build or a major renovation, ventilation should be considered at design stage, alongside insulation, airtightness, heating and ductwork. Design-stage ductwork planning helps ensure that supply and extract routes are practical, that duct sizes and valve positions support the intended airflow, and that the unit is located where it can be serviced. After installation, room-by-room commissioning checks the airflow at each supply and extract point against the design, so that the system is not only installed but also set up to work as intended. Records of the design and commissioning can be useful for the homeowner, the builder and any later servicing, because they show what was intended for each room. Coordination between the designer, builder and MVHR installer is important, because changes to layouts, ceiling details or airtightness measures can affect ventilation routes and performance.

Living in an airtight home with MVHR

Day-to-day use matters as much as design. Occupants should understand the normal and boost settings, how to use controls for cooking and showering, and how to look after filters in line with the manufacturer guidance. Filters need attention so that incoming air remains filtered and airflow is not restricted, and the unit and ductwork should remain accessible for servicing. Opening windows remains possible for additional fresh air, but it should not be the only ventilation strategy in an airtight home, because window opening is intermittent and weather dependent, while a planned system provides continuous background ventilation. If a system seems noisy, stuffy or difficult to use, that is a reason to ask for a check of the settings, valves and filters, rather than switching the system off, because switching it off removes the planned ventilation that the airtight home relies on.
